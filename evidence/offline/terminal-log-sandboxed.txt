answer:
To determine a post-money and pre-money valuation using the Venture Capital Method, the process involves the following steps [S6]:

1. Determine what a firm thinks a company will be worth at exit (anticipated post-money valuation) through market conditions and industry multiples [S6].
2. Divide this number by the anticipated return on investment (ROI) to arrive at a current post-money valuation [S6].
3. After subtracting the investment amount requested, the investor can arrive at an appropriate pre-money valuation [S6].

Examples from the evidence include:

*   For a healthcare startup, Syntax Health, an investment of $500,000 results in a pre-money valuation of $2.2 million [S2].
*   In a case, the required ownership was 20% of the company, and the post-money valuation was $14.6 million, leading to a pre-money valuation of $9.6 million [S4].

answer:
Venture debt is a tool that allows a company to meet sales demand without giving away equity [S1]. Venture debt is a fairly common tool that lets a company meet sales demand without diluting equity [S1]. Companies may consider venture debt after they have raised a round and need additional cash to accelerate growth [S1].

A company may use venture debt to receive cash without giving up equity [S3]. Debt holders get paid out before equity holders when using debt financing [S3]. Debt financing can be used to purchase an asset, such as a majority of a business, and can help startups receive cash without diluting equity [S3].

Venture debt can be seen as a more viable option for firms and companies depending on the terms [S4]. It allows a company to accelerate toward the next milestone with quick cash and increases the likelihood of reaching that milestone [S5]. This is done while minimizing the equity dilution that would occur with another round of equity financing [S5]. Entrepreneurs can understand the value of venture debt through their retained ownership of equity that would otherwise be lost with another venture round [S5].
